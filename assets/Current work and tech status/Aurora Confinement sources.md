## Current problem

### 1\. Research-grade fusion infrastructure is scarce and expensive

As of 2025, the IAEA counted almost 150 experimental fusion devices and testing facilities worldwide, including facilities that were merely planned or under construction, and more than 20 proposed fusion-plant designs.  
[https\://conferences.iaea.org/event/392/attachments/16840/36163/FEC\_2025\_\_\_Programme\_\_\_Conference\_Material-final.pdf](https://conferences.iaea.org/event/392/attachments/16840/36163/FEC_2025___Programme___Conference_Material-final.pdf)

By comparison, UNESCO reports more than 22,000 accredited higher-education institutions worldwide. Many of the IAEA-listed devices belong to national laboratories and private companies rather than universities.  
[https\://www\.unesco.org/en/articles/transforming-higher-education-global-roadmap-future](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/transforming-higher-education-global-roadmap-future)

The U.S. Government Accountability Office concludes that fusion test facilities “require significant investment and years to build and commission.”  
[https\://www\.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105813](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105813)

The U.S. fusion roadmap classifies major proposed fusion facilities as large-scale when their estimated cost exceeds \$100 million, while also calling for more small- and medium-scale facilities and test stands.  
[https\://www\.energy.gov/articles/fusion-st-roadmap](https://www.energy.gov/articles/fusion-st-roadmap)

Research-grade fusion infrastructure is therefore limited to a small global pool of capital-intensive, specialized facilities.

### 2\. Universities have insufficient access to hands-on experimental platforms

The U.S. National Academies found that major fusion-related facilities are limited in number and under high demand, meaning they cannot provide hands-on training for the entire future workforce. The report identifies broader access to mid-scale facilities as important for training students and scientists.  
[https\://www\.nationalacademies.org/read/26728/chapter/6](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26728/chapter/6)

More than 60 U.S. universities have fusion or plasma-physics programs, but the DOE reported only two national fusion user facilities; one had been undergoing repairs and upgrades since 2016 at the time of the assessment.  
[https\://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107037/index.html](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107037/index.html)

The National Academies separately found that opportunities for new small- and intermediate-scale magnetic-fusion facilities had been “very few” during the preceding decade. It recommended university-sited facilities to strengthen scientific leadership, research visibility, and workforce development.  
[https\://www\.nationalacademies.org/read/25802/chapter/8](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25802/chapter/8)

An analysis presented to the IAEA covering fusion education at 100 European universities identified a need to make fusion curricula more accessible.  
[https\://conferences.iaea.org/event/449/timetable/?view=standard\_numbered](https://conferences.iaea.org/event/449/timetable/?view=standard_numbered)

Many universities therefore teach fusion and plasma physics without having local access to experimental confinement platforms suitable for practical student training and early-stage research.

### 3\. Startups face substantial barriers to experimental testing

The DOE created the INFUSE programme specifically to reduce impediments preventing private fusion companies from accessing national-laboratory and university capabilities. Its projects provide access to otherwise difficult-to-obtain expertise, diagnostics, modelling, materials research, and experimental facilities.  
[https\://www\.energy.gov/science/articles/department-energy-announces-5-million-fusion-research-public-private-partnerships](https://www.energy.gov/science/articles/department-energy-announces-5-million-fusion-research-public-private-partnerships)

Individual INFUSE collaborations were expected to cost \$100,000–\$500,000, with participating companies contributing at least 20%. This illustrates the resources required even when a startup uses existing public infrastructure rather than building its own.  
[https\://www\.energy.gov/science/articles/department-energy-announces-5-million-fusion-research-public-private-partnerships](https://www.energy.gov/science/articles/department-energy-announces-5-million-fusion-research-public-private-partnerships)

The National Academies reports that most private fusion developers have had to access expertise through national laboratories, universities, direct contracts, hiring public-sector specialists, or government partnership programmes. It recommends expanding private-industry access to public experts and facilities.  
[https\://www\.nationalacademies.org/read/25991/chapter/6](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25991/chapter/6)

The DOE’s 2026 roadmap calls infrastructure a primary mechanism for reducing commercial risk and proposes shared test stands, public-private facilities, and access mechanisms that allow companies to test components without independently duplicating specialized infrastructure.  
[https\://www\.energy.gov/fusion/articles/fusion-st-roadmap](https://www.energy.gov/fusion/articles/fusion-st-roadmap)

Early-stage fusion companies therefore often depend on costly collaborations with universities and national laboratories because specialized experimental capabilities are difficult to build or access independently.

### 4\. Fusion remains poorly understood by the public

In a representative 2026 UK government survey of approximately 3,400 people, 65% had heard of fusion, but only 19% knew a fair amount or a lot, and just 5% said they knew a lot.  
[https\://www\.gov.uk/government/statistics/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-spring-2026/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-energy-infrastructure-and-energy-security-spring-2026-uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-spring-2026/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-energy-infrastructure-and-energy-security-spring-2026-uk)

Support was strongly associated with knowledge: 85% of respondents who knew a fair amount or a lot supported fusion development, compared with 26% of those unaware of it. This is an association, not proof that knowledge causes support, but it shows the importance of understanding.  
[https\://www\.gov.uk/government/statistics/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-spring-2026/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-energy-infrastructure-and-energy-security-spring-2026-uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-spring-2026/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-energy-infrastructure-and-energy-security-spring-2026-uk)

A joint UK–Japan–Canada regulatory working group concluded: “Public knowledge about fusion is generally low.” It warned that fusion could struggle to gain acceptance and reach meaningful deployment if the public lacks confidence in its regulation and safety.  
[https\://www\.gov.uk/government/publications/agile-nations-uk-japan-and-canada-joint-recommendations-on-fusion-energy/agile-nations-working-group-on-fusion-energy-regulation-initial-recommendations](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/agile-nations-uk-japan-and-canada-joint-recommendations-on-fusion-energy/agile-nations-working-group-on-fusion-energy-regulation-initial-recommendations)

Awareness of fusion is therefore considerably broader than meaningful understanding, making the technology difficult to communicate and public confidence harder to establish.

### 5\. These access gaps create commercialisation risks

Innovation: The GAO reports that researchers still lack sufficient experimental data to validate important fusion simulations. It also found that no facility existed where candidate materials could be fully tested under all expected fusion conditions.  
[https\://www\.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105813](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105813)

Talent: The GAO identifies shortages of fusion scientists, nuclear engineers, and electrical engineers. It notes that training engineers, technicians, and physicists takes years while funding and training opportunities remain limited.  
[https\://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107037/index.html](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107037/index.html)

Industry growth: The Fusion Industry Association’s 2026 survey covered 56 fusion companies and reported more than 16,000 people employed across the sector, alongside \$14.24 billion in reported cumulative funding. These are industry-reported figures.  
[https\://www\.fusionindustryassociation.org/fusion-industry-attracts-record-annual-funding-of-4-48bn-raising-total-to-14-24bn/](https://www.fusionindustryassociation.org/fusion-industry-attracts-record-annual-funding-of-4-48bn-raising-total-to-14-24bn/)

Public confidence: The United States and Japan explicitly identify public engagement as necessary to create a “social license” for fusion deployment.  
[https\://www\.energy.gov/articles/joint-statement-between-doe-and-japan-ministry-education-sports-science-and-technology](https://www.energy.gov/articles/joint-statement-between-doe-and-japan-ministry-education-sports-science-and-technology)

Limited access to experimental infrastructure constrains training and validation, while low public understanding creates an additional deployment risk. Governments now identify infrastructure, workforce, and community perception as interconnected fusion-commercialisation challenges.

### 6\. Europe faces a fragmented fusion ecosystem and commercialisation gap

The European Commission says that “a coherent EU-level approach should be adopted” to facilitate research and technology innovation, encourage private-sector engagement, de-risk investment where necessary, and enable cooperation between private companies and publicly funded research organisations.  
[https\://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/research-and-technology/fusion-energy-and-iter\_en](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/research-and-technology/fusion-energy-and-iter_en)

The Commission, citing the Draghi report on European competitiveness, identifies the need to create “a stable and predictable fusion ecosystem for industrial innovation,” supported by a clear technology-development roadmap.  
[https\://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/research-and-technology/fusion-energy-and-iter\_en](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/research-and-technology/fusion-energy-and-iter_en)

The European Commission states that global competition is growing and notes that the United States, United Kingdom, China, and Japan have already launched national fusion strategies. The EU’s first dedicated Fusion Strategy is planned for publication in 2026\.  
[https\://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-launches-call-evidence-support-first-ever-eu-wide-fusion-strategy-2025-06-06\_en](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-launches-call-evidence-support-first-ever-eu-wide-fusion-strategy-2025-06-06_en)

The Commission’s proposed EU strategy includes a workforce strategy to address skills shortages, stronger private-sector participation and public-private partnerships, new funding mechanisms, development of the European fusion supply chain, an enabling regulatory framework, and more effective EU-level governance and decision-making.  
[https\://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-launches-call-evidence-support-first-ever-eu-wide-fusion-strategy-2025-06-06\_en](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-launches-call-evidence-support-first-ever-eu-wide-fusion-strategy-2025-06-06_en)

A 2024 European Commission study found that EU fusion financing had historically been led primarily by public bodies investing in large projects, while industry generally played a more passive role as a supplier of components and services. The Commission says a new strategic approach should use public-private partnerships to close technology gaps, strengthen industrial investment, and prioritise innovation and technology transfer.  
[https\://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/new-commission-study-potential-public-private-partnership-approach-foster-innovation-fusion-energy-2024-03-07\_en](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/new-commission-study-potential-public-private-partnership-approach-foster-innovation-fusion-energy-2024-03-07_en)

Implication for Aurora Confinement: These European Commission findings do not by themselves prove that every European university or startup lacks experimental access. They do show that Europe’s fusion capabilities, funding, industrial participation, workforce development, and governance are not yet connected into a sufficiently coherent commercial ecosystem. Limited access to affordable experimental platforms can reinforce this gap by making it harder for more universities and startups to train people, begin practical research, validate ideas, and participate in fusion innovation.

## Product C Research Platform

### Product Overview and Intended Use

#### Description:

The **Aurora Confinement Turnkey Research Stellarator (Model C)** is a fully integrated, modular plasma physics platform. Designed around a highly customizable permanent-magnet and toroidal field coil architecture, this compact stellarator provides a functional, UHV-capable (Ultra-High Vacuum) experimental environment. It features a complete ECRH (Electron Cyclotron Resonance Heating) microwave system, automated gas handling for Argon/Helium/Hydrogen plasmas, and an integrated diagnostic suite including Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES) and high-speed data acquisition.  
A key feature of the platform is its **high degree of configurability**. Researchers can tailor the magnetic configuration to investigate different stellarator topologies, magnetic field geometries, and plasma confinement concepts. The system can also be configured as a flexible testbed for validating newly developed diagnostics, sensors, and experimental instrumentation. To support studies across multiple magnetic configurations, the ECRH heating system can be repositioned and adapted to account for changes in the plasma axis location, enabling consistent plasma generation across a range of experimental setups.

#### Target Buyers:

* University Plasma Physics Departments: Used to upgrade from legacy theoretical courses to hands-on, experimental plasma physics programs.  
* National Laboratories: Deployed as an agile, off-critical-path platform to validate new diagnostic tools or test localized magnetic error-field theories without consuming time on multi-billion-euro national machines.  
* Allied Fusion Startups: Used to train new hires, test component durability, or prototype custom control software before deploying on their flagship reactors.

#### Intended Purpose of Use:

To drastically lower the barrier to entry for experimental stellarator research. By providing a reconfigurable, "plug-and-play" magnetic confinement platform, Product C allows institutions to immediately begin researching turbulence, transport, and 3D magnetic topology without the massive capital expenditure and multi-year lead time of building a bespoke stellarator from scratch.

### 9\. Validation plan and investor interpretation

## Climate impact and SDG’s

### 1\. Impact position

Aurora Confinement does not generate fusion electricity, and Product B cannot presently be assigned direct avoided greenhouse-gas emissions. Its near-term impact is enabling and educational: making real plasma visible, supporting introductory science learning, and creating a more concrete basis for informed discussion about fusion.  
Aurora implication: education and engagement outcomes must be measured separately from environmental outcomes. Visitor numbers, teaching hours, and changes in understanding must not be converted into speculative tonnes of avoided CO₂.

### 2\. Long-term climate relevance of fusion

The European Nuclear Safety Regulators Group states that a fusion power plant would produce no carbon dioxide during operation. It also stresses that sustained fusion is technically difficult and that ITER is an experimental project rather than a plant supplying electricity to the grid.  
[https\://nuclear-safety-regulators.ec.europa.eu/topics/fusion-energy\_en](https://nuclear-safety-regulators.ec.europa.eu/topics/fusion-energy_en)

ITER explains that fusion systems can still generate activated materials when energetic neutrons interact with reactor components. These materials require controlled handling and waste management even though their waste profile differs from conventional fission.  
[https\://www\.iter.org/faqs?thematic=77](https://www.iter.org/faqs?thematic=77)

Aurora implication: commercially viable fusion could eventually contribute to a low-carbon energy system, but its timing, cost, deployment scale, and resulting emissions reductions remain uncertain. Product B contributes only indirectly by supporting education and engagement around this developing technology.

### 3\. Product B’s direct environmental footprint

Product B’s environmental footprint arises from its glass vessel, aluminium and structural materials, copper windings, permanent magnets, electronics, safety enclosure, packaging, transport, electricity use, and end-of-life treatment. The noble gas and any other consumables must also be documented once the engineering design is fixed.

Aurora should favour a durable and repairable design with replaceable electronics, modular components, documented maintenance, reusable transport protection, and an end-of-life recovery route. These are design objectives and must be validated during prototype development.

### 4\. Early impact measurement framework

Environmental indicators: electricity consumption per demonstration hour; estimated embodied carbon per unit; quantities of key materials and consumables; expected operating lifetime; repairability and module-replacement rate; transport distance; packaging recovery; and percentage of materials recoverable at end of life.

Education and engagement indicators: institutions using Product B; demonstrations delivered; students and visitors reached; teaching hours enabled; staff trained; partnerships established; and measured change in participant understanding before and after a demonstration where feasible.

Commercial and validation indicators: functioning prototype milestone; bill of materials; safety requirements; institutional interviews; demonstrations with target users; letters of interest; paid pilots; validated price range; and documented route to installation and support.

### 5\. Contribution to the UN Sustainable Development Goals

The United Nations defines SDG 4 around quality education, SDG 7 around affordable and clean energy, SDG 9 around infrastructure and innovation, SDG 13 around climate action, and SDG 17 around partnerships. Aurora can contribute to these goals, but should not claim that a single product achieves them.  
[https\://sdgs.un.org/goals](https://sdgs.un.org/goals)

SDG 4 \-  Quality Education. Activity: hands-on demonstrations and teaching material using visible plasma. Intended outcome: improved introductory plasma literacy. Early indicators: learners reached, teaching hours, participating institutions, and measured learning outcomes.

SDG 7 \- Affordable and Clean Energy. Activity: engagement with the science behind a potential future low-carbon energy technology. Intended outcome: greater capacity to understand fusion’s opportunities and limitations. Early indicators: demonstrations, courses supported, and institutions incorporating the material into energy education. This is an enabling contribution, not evidence of current clean-energy production.

SDG 9 \- Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure. Activity: development of accessible scientific demonstration equipment and collaboration with museums, universities, startups, and laboratories. Intended outcome: stronger science-engagement capability and a validated Danish deep-tech product. Early indicators: prototype maturity, partnerships, pilots, and institutional customers.

SDG 13 \- Climate Action. Activity: climate and energy education that presents both fusion’s potential and its uncertainties. Intended outcome: better-informed discussion of long-term decarbonisation options. Early indicators: climate-related learning modules and measured understanding. Aurora should not report avoided emissions at this stage.

SDG 17 \- Partnerships for the Goals. Activity: cooperation among museums, schools, universities, startups, research institutions, and public organisations. Intended outcome: shared demonstrations, teaching content, and validation. Early indicators: formal partnerships, letters of interest, co-development activities, and jointly delivered events.

### 6\. Impact conclusion for the pre-seed case

Product B’s credible present impact is not that it accelerates fusion deployment by a quantifiable number of years. It is that it can make an otherwise abstract technology visible, support science education, and provide institutions with a practical engagement tool. The DKK 100,000 validation round should establish whether this benefit is technically deliverable, wanted by customers, and environmentally responsible.
